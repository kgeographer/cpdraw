Yes. And I think the technological situation has changed enough in the last couple of years that this is worth revisiting seriously. What you are describing is no longer primarily a “computer vision extracts lines from a scan” problem. It can be formulated as a collaborative system in which a vision-language model understands the cartographic problem, specialized vision models identify the graphical objects, conventional geometry software constructs valid vectors, and a human concentrates on ambiguous cases rather than tracing everything.

The important distinction is that I would not ask the LLM itself to trace the boundary. LLM/VLM output coordinates are not metrically reliable enough for that. I would give it a role much closer to that of a GIS technician supervising a collection of specialized tools.

A possible pipeline would look roughly like this:

The system receives a high-resolution map, ideally already georeferenced, although georeferencing could itself be another stage.

A VLM examines the map globally: title, legend, colors, line styles, political units, inset maps, neatline, graticule, labels, rivers, roads, etc. You could say, for example, “extract the boundaries of the political entities represented in red, but ignore province boundaries shown by dashed lines.”

The VLM then creates prompts or training examples for a segmentation model. This is where something quite remarkable has happened. A paper published just this month, August 2026, describes SMOL-MapSeg, which uses what the authors call legend-oriented prompting: essentially, “here is the graphical symbol in the legend; find this class everywhere on the map.” That strikes me as extraordinarily relevant to your problem because it approximates what a human map reader actually does. 

A segmentation model produces raster masks or boundary probability surfaces. SAM-derived systems are already being adapted specifically to historical maps. MapSAM, published in 2025, was designed to overcome the poor zero-shot performance of ordinary Segment Anything on historical cartography, and the authors demonstrated it on railways, vineyards, and building blocks using very small amounts of training data. 

Geometry software converts the segmentation into vectors and enforces topology: snapping shared boundaries, eliminating tiny gaps and slivers, closing rings, resolving self-intersections, simplifying excessive vertices, identifying enclaves, etc. This is an area where conventional GIS algorithms remain much more appropriate than an LLM.

The VLM performs a second interpretive pass. It can compare the resulting overlay with the original image and say, in effect, “this section crossed a text label and the extracted boundary has jumped to the coastline,” or “these two polygons should share the same boundary.” It can request re-segmentation of only that small region.

The resulting polygons get geographic coordinates from the map transformation and can be emitted as GeoJSON, GeoPackage, PostGIS geometry, or whatever else you want.

There is already research pointing directly toward the vector/topology part as well. Xia and colleagues published a 2024 approach specifically called “Vectorizing historical maps with topological consistency.” Instead of merely classifying pixels and then blindly polygonizing them, their system predicts contours assembled from geometric primitives and aims explicitly at producing simplified, nonintersecting polygon geometry. Their application was Sanborn building footprints rather than historical political boundaries, but the underlying problem—turning uncertain raster evidence into structurally valid polygon geometry—is very close to yours. 

And there is an encouraging precedent for doing this at collection scale. mapKurator has already processed more than 60,000 historical maps and extracted more than 100 million textual labels, producing GeoJSON results. It is doing text rather than boundary geometry, but it demonstrates that a pipeline combining machine learning, very large images, postprocessing and GIS output is operationally feasible at archival scale. 
 MapReader likewise provides an existing Python framework for breaking huge map scans into patches, annotating examples, training models, running them over collections and creating structured output. 

What strikes me as particularly promising about the LLM/VLM contribution is that it could attack one of the things that has made this problem so resistant to conventional automation: cartographic heterogeneity.

A computer-vision pipeline trained to recognize the borders on one nineteenth-century atlas may fail spectacularly on another atlas because the line weights, colors, hachures, engraving methods and printing quality differ. But a multimodal model can reason from the particular map in front of it. It can see that “this solid green line is apparently the international boundary, this finer dotted line is administrative, blue is hydrography, and the thick black line is the railroad.” SMOL-MapSeg's legend-oriented approach is essentially exploiting precisely this idea algorithmically. 

There is another aspect I think is especially important for the corpus you have in mind. I wouldn't initially try to build a universal historical-boundary extractor. I would build a human-guided extraction agent.

You open a map. You tell it, “These are the boundaries I'm interested in.” Perhaps you click three or four examples. The agent shows you an overlay. You correct two places. It reruns. Then it exports polygons with a confidence measure and perhaps marks twenty uncertain stretches of boundary for inspection.

That changes the economics enormously.

Suppose manual tracing takes an hour per map. An imperfect automatic method that requires forty minutes of cleanup isn't very interesting. But one that gets 95% of the geometry right and asks a knowledgeable person to adjudicate ten ambiguous locations might turn an hour into five minutes. At a million-map scale, that distinction is transformative.

I would also preserve something that most GIS digitization workflows tend to throw away: provenance and uncertainty at the segment level. The product shouldn't merely be:

POLYGON(...)

It could retain something more like:

geometry → source map → pixel coordinates → map coordinates → extraction model → confidence → corrections → operator → interpretation

And particular arcs could carry lower confidence where, say, a label obscures the boundary. That would make this vastly more useful scholarly data than anonymously digitized polygons.

There is one conceptual complication that I think becomes fascinating rather than troublesome in this context. A political region is not necessarily represented cartographically as a polygon. The map may show only some of its borders because a coastline supplies another boundary; two territories may share a single line; boundaries can disappear under type; colored washes may overlap; dashed boundaries may indicate uncertainty; the map may represent contested sovereignty through annotation rather than geometry. So the system has to understand cartographic semantics, not merely linework.

And that is exactly where the VLM belongs.

In fact, I would divide the problem into three intellectual layers:

Seeing: Which pixels constitute the cartographic feature?

Constructing: What geometrical/topological object do those pixels imply?

Interpreting: What does that object mean on this particular map?

Traditional computer vision has concentrated overwhelmingly on the first. GIS algorithms handle the second fairly well. The third has usually required a human. Multimodal LLMs suddenly give us a plausible computational participant in that third layer.

So yes—I think an agent could be built now. And I suspect a proof of concept could be considerably smaller than one might initially imagine: perhaps 20–50 maps from two or three reasonably distinct historical atlases, extracting just one class such as first-order political boundaries, with a browser/QGIS-style interface for correction.

The experiment I would most want to run is almost embarrassingly simple: take perhaps ten representative maps for which good hand-digitized boundary geometry already exists, and see how far one can get with VLM interpretation + MapSAM/SMOL-style segmentation + topology-aware polygonization + VLM-assisted QA, without training a large bespoke model.

If that worked at all well, then I think you would have the beginnings of something quite important rather than merely another historical-map extraction experiment.

And there's an interface-design lesson there that I hadn't quite appreciated until you brought this up. Perhaps you don't even want to require the user to crudely trace the whole polygon initially.

Imagine clicking inside a colored historical region.

The system immediately proposes an extent, Apple-style. If it's right: accept.

If it leaks into the neighboring polity, click there: “not this.”

If it missed an enclave: click inside the enclave: “include this.”

If segmentation simply can't figure it out, then Draw asks you to sketch an approximate boundary.

That gives you a hierarchy of human effort rather than always demanding 20 points:

click → inspect → correct with clicks → rough trace → manual editing

The easy cartographic objects become almost instantaneous. Human labor gets progressively introduced only as the visual ambiguity increases.

And that is essentially the interaction model behind Meta's Segment Anything work. SAM was specifically designed around prompts—points, boxes, masks—rather than requiring the user to manually delineate an object. SAM 2 continues that paradigm and is explicitly capable of refining a segmentation through additional prompts. Meta Segment Anything 2 research page

There is another implication that I think matters a great deal for your project. The interface itself may be as important a research contribution as the segmentation model. You probably don't need to invent a dramatically better computer-vision model. There are people whose entire careers are devoted to that problem. What seems much less developed is figuring out the optimal interaction between a historian/geographer looking at a map and these increasingly capable segmentation models.

Your Draw application could essentially become an experimental workbench for answering that question: What is the minimum human gesture necessary to turn a historical cartographic representation into trustworthy geometry?

That is a rather different—and, to me, considerably more interesting—problem than “use AI to vectorize old maps.”
